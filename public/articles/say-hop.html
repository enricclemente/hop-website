Say hop.

A letter to Poland’s best companies

Philip Jacobson and David Anson · 11 November 2026 · 6 min read

Relax. You’ve already done the hard part.

Thirty years ago this country was building almost from scratch. Today you make the sofa in the Stockholm showroom, the bus gliding through Barcelona, the yacht moored in Monaco. You write the code inside apps used by millions. You run services so good that visitors go home and wonder why theirs are so bad.

You won the race on quality. Congratulations. Nobody knows.

01The best-kept secret in Europe is not a compliment.

Somewhere right now, a German family is unpacking something you made. They love it. They’ll recommend it to their friends. They’ll never say your name, because it isn’t on the box.

That isn’t modesty. It’s a business model, and its ceiling is low. When you sell capacity, the buyer sets your price. When you sell a name, you do. One of those can be undercut by a factory somewhere cheaper next year. The other can’t.

02Europe doesn’t know what it’s missing.

Try splitting a dinner bill in London. Someone asks for a sort code. Someone else promises to “sort it later.” In Warsaw, it’s done before the waiter is back: six digits, BLIK, gone. Why doesn’t every Brit have that? Why is the country that runs Europe’s financial capital still making people type in account numbers?

Now try finding a cold drink and a decent sandwich in a German city on a Sunday evening. Anyone who has been to one recently knows what we mean. Here, there’s a Żabka on the corner: open late, hot food, coffee, your parcel waiting behind the counter. In Germany, there’s a closed shutter and a long walk.

These aren’t charming local quirks. They’re better products and better services.

03Used by millions. Known by nobody.

It’s worse in tech. Booksy books appointments for barbers and salons far beyond Poland. PayPo lets millions of shoppers pay later. Pocket sits in on your meetings and remembers everything so you don’t have to. Millions of people use them.

Ask those people about the brand, and most will shrug. Ask them where it’s from, and nobody will say Poland.

That’s the gap. Not the product, not the service, not the technology. The name, the story, and the nerve to tell it.

04The stories that keep you the subcontractor.

Poles don’t do brands. We’re not ready. Foreigners won’t pay a premium for Polish. Let’s grow a bit more first.

You’ve heard them all. You’ve probably said a few yourself. They sound like caution. They’re actually fear in a good suit.

These stories survive because they’re comfortable. They let you skip the meeting where someone has to ask what you stand for. They let you keep winning tenders on price and calling it a strategy. And they keep you in the shadows. You make it; they own the name, set the price and take the margin. They were handed down by a generation that had every reason to keep its head down. You don’t.

That’s the difference between a subcontractor and a margin owner. It isn’t the factory. It’s the story.

Quality is the entry ticket. It is not the prize.

Nobody queues outside a shop because the stitching is good. They queue because of what the thing says about them. Quality earns you a seat at the table. A brand is what makes people save you one.

You already have the hard, expensive, unglamorous part: the product. What’s missing is the cheap, fast, brave part: the belief.

How is it done? Ask the Italians. For them, craft isn’t a step in the process. It is the process. The difference is that they never stop telling you about it.

05Don’t be Wokulski.

Prus wrote the warning nearly 140 years ago. Wokulski had the money, the drive and the talent. What he lacked was the conviction that he belonged in the room. He spent his life trying to be accepted by people who should have been trying to impress him.

Too many Polish companies are still waiting at that door. Stop knocking. Build your own room.

06A woman named an element after a country that wasn’t on the map.

In 1898, Poland didn’t exist as a state. Maria Skłodowska-Curie helped discover a new element anyway and named it polonium, so the whole world would have to say the name of a place it had erased.

That’s the most audacious act of national branding in history. She didn’t wait for permission, or a better moment, or for Poland to be ready.

The instinct survives. In 2022, two school friends from Warsaw founded an AI voice company and named it ElevenLabs, after 11 November, the day Poland regained its independence. Its voices now speak dozens of languages around the world, and most of the people listening have no idea the name is a Polish date.

Neither of them waited. Neither should you.

07It’s been done. Recently. By people who started further back.

Within living memory, “Made in Korea” meant cheap. Then a generation of Korean companies decided they were done being the factory behind other people’s brands. Now the world watches their dramas, sings their songs and carries their phones.

It’s already starting here. CD Projekt took a Polish fantasy saga and made it a global obsession. Tylko took furniture, the thing Poland has always made for other people, and reinvented it, so that anyone could have a home that fits them to the centimetre. Under its own name. Inglot put Polish colour on counters around the world.

BLIK, Żabka, Booksy and PayPo could be next. They’ve built the product. Now they need to build the story.

Brands like these don’t just sell things. They change, materially and psychologically, what “Made in Poland” means. And by extension, what Poland means.

08A generation with nothing to prove.

Ask young Poles whether Poland still needs to catch up with the West. Nearly three in four will tell you it doesn’t. Four in five think other countries could learn from us. Three in four say they choose Polish when they can.

Their parents grew up measuring Poland against somewhere else. They don’t. They’ve seen London and Berlin, and they come home glad that everything works.

This is the first generation validated to dream. Not tolerated. Not permitted. Validated by the cities they live in, the services they use and the companies they’re proud of.

The country has changed. The story hasn’t caught up. Your customers, your engineers and your next hires already believe a Polish name can be a prestige name. Too many Polish brands are still talking to them as if it were 2005.

Competing on price and staying invisible was a great model for the last thirty years. It’s not a model for the next ten.

And the stakes are bigger than margin. When a Polish name becomes a prestige name, wages rise, talent stays and people come home. Fewer Poles leave for Germany now than come back. Prestige isn’t vanity. It changes lives.

09So here’s what to do on Monday.

  1. 01Decide how you want to be remembered.Not this quarter. In thirty years. Everything else follows from the answer.
  2. 02Know who you’re for, and love them more than anyone else does.Understand what they’re thinking and feeling, not just what they’re doing.
  3. 03Be the best solution.Not the cheapest. Not the safest. The one people would miss if it disappeared.
  4. 04Make them feel something.Being the best solution gets you considered. Feeling is what gets you chosen.
  5. 05Build a brand ready for the world.If your story doesn’t work outside Poland, you need a different story.
  6. 06Pick a fight.Own the one idea that makes the right people love you and the wrong people uncomfortable.
  7. 07Be proud out loud.The doubt that makes you check everything three times is a gift. Keep the doubt. Lose the whisper.
  8. 08Ask what the world says about you.Then decide what you want it to say, and spend only on closing the gap.

And put your name on the box.

10Nie mów hop, póki nie przeskoczysz.

Every Pole grows up with it: don’t say hop until you’ve jumped. It’s good advice for crossing a ditch. It’s terrible advice for building a brand.

A brand is saying hop before you’ve landed. It’s a promise made out loud, before the proof is in. Every great brand in the world started as a claim slightly bigger than the company making it.

Our time is now. So we’re asking Poland’s founders, CEOs and boards to do something our grandmothers warned us against.

Hop.Phil Jacobson and David Anson
